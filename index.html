Teach Your Body to Read Wanted Posters

My immune system is a police force that patrols every inch of me, every day, unpaid. Cancer cells get away with it because they look like ordinary citizens. So the plan is simple: print wanted posters, show the cops exactly who to arrest, then go back to the couch.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

This is a real field, called immunotherapy. In CAR-T therapy, doctors take a patient's own T cells, engineer them to recognise a marker on cancer cells and put them back; the first one was approved in the US in 2017, for a childhood leukaemia. Checkpoint inhibitors, drugs that take the brakes off immune cells, won their discoverers the 2018 Nobel Prize in medicine. Personalised mRNA cancer vaccines, posters printed from a patient's own tumour, are in clinical trials right now.

Why it is

Cancer isn't one criminal, it's thousands, and they change their faces. Solid tumours build walls the cops struggle to get through, these treatments can trigger dangerous immune over-reactions, and some cost hundreds of thousands of dollars per patient. “Cure cancer” really means curing a few hundred different diseases.

Step one

Book the screening you've been putting off. The cheapest wanted poster is the one printed early.

Replace Money With a Ledger of Favours

Since I moved here with five shirts, almost nobody has paid me in money, but I've been paid plenty: soup from the lady downstairs, a lease translated by the guy whose bike I fixed. What if the whole economy ran like that? One big notebook of who did what for whom, no coins.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

Time banks exist: members trade hours of help, one hour for one hour, whoever you are. The idea was popularised by Edgar Cahn in the US in the 1980s. Japan's Fureai Kippu lets people earn credits by caring for elderly people, which they can save for later or send to a parent in another city. And the anthropologist David Graeber argued that credit and IOUs are older than coins; nobody has found a society that ran on pure barter.

Why it is

One hour of brain surgery is not one hour of me playing guitar badly, however heartfelt the chorus. A ledger between neighbours works because everyone knows everyone. Strangers on different continents need something more boring to trust, and that boring thing is called money.

Step one

Offer one hour to a neighbour this week. Write it down. Do not remind them.

End Traffic by Building Cities for Feet

Every time a city gets a traffic jam, someone adds a lane, and then the lane fills up. I propose the opposite: put the bakery, the school and the doctor close enough to walk to, and let the street become a park that occasionally lets a bike through.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

Economists call the lane thing induced demand. A 2011 study by Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner found that in US cities, driving rose roughly in step with new road capacity. Barcelona's superblocks close groups of streets to through-traffic and hand them back to people. Pontevedra in Spain began pedestrianising its centre in 1999, and Paris took most cars off the Rue de Rivoli in 2020.

Why it is

Most of us already live in places designed around cars, and buildings last a hundred years. Deliveries, ambulances and people who can't walk far still need to get in. And every car-free street begins with a public meeting where a man shouts.

Step one

Walk to one errand this week. Notice what's missing on the way, a bench, a crossing, some shade, and tell the council. They do read those. Eventually.

Learn to Talk to Whales

Sperm whales talk in clicks. Humpbacks sing long songs that change over the years and spread from group to group like a hit single. I want a phrasebook. Hello. Sorry about the whaling. Love the new album.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

Project CETI, started in 2020, records sperm whales off Dominica and uses machine learning to study their click patterns, called codas. In 2024 its researchers published a study arguing that the codas combine in structured ways, more like an alphabet than anyone expected. And whale song has changed history once already: Roger Payne's 1970 album Songs of the Humpback Whale helped start the Save the Whales movement, and in 1982 the International Whaling Commission voted for a moratorium on commercial whaling.

Why it is

Decoding patterns isn't the same as knowing what they mean. A sperm whale's world is dark, deep and full of squid; we may have almost nothing in common to talk about. I can't even order coffee here.

Step one

Listen. Put on a humpback recording tonight with the lights off. You won't understand a word, which is exactly how I feel at the post office.

Body · 1 min read ×

Make Sleep Optional

Imagine eight extra hours a day. I'd learn the language. Find a job. Iron all five shirts. The plan: work out how to skip sleep safely, and give everyone a second shift of being awake.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

Some people really do need less. In 2009 a team led by Ying-Hui Fu found a mutation in a gene called DEC2 in a mother and daughter who felt rested on about six hours a night, and the same lab has since found other “natural short sleeper” genes. Dolphins and some birds rest one half of the brain at a time, so they can keep swimming or flying.

Why it is

Sleep isn't idle time. A 2013 study found that the brains of sleeping mice clear out waste products, including a protein linked to Alzheimer's, much faster than when awake. Regularly sleeping too little is linked to worse health in study after study. Short-sleeper genes are rare, and nobody knows how to give them to the rest of us.

Step one

Go to bed. This is the only project on the blog where step one is lying down, and it counts.

Cure Loneliness With Benches

My first night here I sat on a bench and talked to nobody. Turns out plenty of the people walking past would have liked to talk to somebody too. So: a worldwide network of benches with one rule. If you sit here, you're open for a chat.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

It already exists in small doses. “Happy to Chat” benches began in Cardiff in 2019 with a simple sign, and the idea spread to other towns. The UK appointed a minister for loneliness in 2018, Japan did in 2021, and in 2023 the US Surgeon General published an advisory calling loneliness an epidemic. In a 2014 study by Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder, train commuters asked to talk to a stranger expected to hate it, then reported a more pleasant ride than those who sat alone.

Why it is

Plenty of cities do the opposite: they remove benches, or add armrests so nobody can lie down. Some people want to be left alone, and they're allowed. And there will always be the man who wants to explain his investment strategy.

Step one

Sit on a bench. Say hello to one person. If you don't share a language, nod with great confidence. It worked for me.

Take the Lift to Space

Run a cable from the equator straight up, past geostationary orbit about 36,000 km above us, to a counterweight. Climbers ride the cable on electricity, no explosions. You press UP, you wait a few days, you're in space. I'd bring a book.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

The idea is older than spaceflight: Konstantin Tsiolkovsky imagined a giant tower in 1895, Yuri Artsutanov described the cable version in 1960, and Arthur C. Clarke made it famous in his 1979 novel The Fountains of Paradise. The physics works. The Japanese construction company Obayashi has published a concept for one. And in 2019 two researchers proposed a “spaceline” from the Moon towards Earth that could be built from materials that already exist.

Why it is

Nothing we can make is strong and light enough for a cable from Earth. Carbon nanotubes might be in theory, but we can't make them long and flawless enough, and we'd need tens of thousands of kilometres. Then add lightning, wind, space debris and the question of who holds the other end.

Step one

Cheer for materials science. Every boring lab making a slightly stronger fibre is, technically, building a lift to space.

Water the Sahara

Most of the planet is water and a big chunk of Africa is sand. The ocean is right there. Desalinate it, pump it inland, plant trees, done. I've been told this is “an oversimplification”. I'm writing it down anyway.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

The Sahara really was green. During the African Humid Period, roughly 11,000 to 5,000 years ago, it had lakes, grasslands, hippos and cave paintings that look a lot like people swimming. The African Union's Great Green Wall, launched in 2007, aims to restore land across the Sahel. And desalination already supplies a large share of drinking water in countries like Israel and Saudi Arabia.

Why it is

Desalination uses a lot of energy and leaves salty brine that has to go somewhere. Pumping water hundreds of kilometres inland into a hot desert is expensive, and much of it would evaporate. Also, Saharan dust blows across the Atlantic and fertilises the Amazon, so greening one could starve the other a little. Everything is connected, man.

Step one

Read about the farmers in Niger regreening their land by letting old tree stumps grow back. Then water your own plant. It's been three weeks.

End War With a Universal Translator

I've lived here for months and still mostly communicate by nodding. An earpiece that turns every language into mine would fix half my problems. Scale it up to the planet and surely nobody starts a war with someone they understand.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

Translation tech moved shockingly fast. In 2022 Meta released NLLB-200, a single model that translates between 200 languages, including many that had almost no tools before. Live speech translation now runs on ordinary phones. And the dream is old: L. L. Zamenhof published Esperanto in 1887 hoping a shared, neutral language would ease hatred between peoples.

Why it is

Wars are rarely started by a mistranslation. Plenty of people who share a language have fought each other; civil wars prove it every decade. Understanding someone's words is not the same as agreeing with them, and machines still fumble tone and nuance, which in diplomacy is most of the job.

Step one

Learn “thank you” in the language of someone near you. My first word here was pilv, cloud. My second was aitäh. It opened more doors.

Abolish Meetings, Globally

I don't even have a job and I've been to three meetings this week, one about the building's recycling bins that lasted ninety minutes. Proposal: no more meetings of more than two people, anywhere, ever, unless something is actually on fire.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

In January 2023 Shopify deleted every recurring meeting with more than two people from its employees' calendars and asked them to think hard before adding any back. Amazon famously starts many meetings with everyone silently reading a six-page memo, so the time goes on thinking rather than slides. The organisational psychologist Steven Rogelberg has spent years studying how much meeting time is wasted, and how to fix it.

Why it is

Some things really do need people in a room: hard decisions, conflict, grief, welcoming the new person. And meetings grow back like weeds; a calendar purge is a reset, not a cure. Also, my building would never agree on the bins by email.

Step one

Decline one meeting this week with a short, kind note that says what you would have said. See if anybody notices.

Let Rocks Eat the Carbon

Rocks look lazy, but some are secretly doing climate work. Basalt and olivine react with carbon dioxide and lock it away as solid minerals. So: grind up mountains, spread them everywhere, let them breathe in our mess.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

This already happens: rock weathering is one of the main ways Earth has regulated its carbon over millions of years. In Iceland, the Carbfix project injects CO₂ dissolved in water into basalt, and a 2016 study found that over 95% of it had turned to stone within two years. Farmers are trialling crushed basalt spread on fields, which may also help some soils.

Why it is

Scale. Humanity emits tens of billions of tonnes of CO₂ a year, while Iceland's Orca capture plant, opened in 2021, was designed to capture about 4,000 tonnes a year. Grinding and moving billions of tonnes of rock takes a lot of energy, and natural weathering is slow. The rocks are on our side; they're just not in a hurry.

Step one

Cut emissions first. The best rock is the one that never has to eat it.

Every City Feeds Itself

My window gets four hours of sun and I've grown exactly one basil. Now imagine every roof, balcony and car park in the city doing that, seriously. No trucks, no plastic, just walk upstairs for dinner.

Why it's not as crazy as it sounds

Cities have done it under pressure. When Cuba lost Soviet imports in the early 1990s, Havana filled with urban gardens called organopónicos, which became a model for city farming worldwide. During the Second World War, “victory gardens” in the US and UK grew a large share of home-front vegetables. In 2019 Singapore set a goal of producing 30% of its nutritional needs locally by 2030.

Why it is

Lettuce is easy; calories are hard. Wheat, rice and potatoes need far more land and sun than a city has. Several high-tech vertical farms have gone bust because lighting plants indoors eats electricity; AeroFarms filed for bankruptcy in 2023. Cities can grow a lot of greens, but probably not their bread.

Step one

Grow one thing you'll actually eat. Basil counts. A second basil counts double.
